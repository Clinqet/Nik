Offer limits — how many uses, how much discount

Provider web + phone, and what the customer sees. A provider can cap an offer two ways: by number of uses and by total discount given. Either one reaching its limit stops the offer everywhere — marketplace, provider page and the phone assistant — automatically. Raising a limit starts it again. Drawn for approval; nothing is built.

The one rule that decides everything on this sheet. An offer is shown when it is under every limit that is set. A limit left blank means “no limit”. Nothing is ever deleted or switched off by the platform — the provider’s own on/off switch stays theirs.

Only an owner or manager can change an offer’s limits.
Why the numbers are shown but not editable. “12 of 50” and “$180 of $500” are counted by the platform, so they are read-only — a provider can never type over them. The limits themselves always stay editable, including while the offer is stopped: that is the whole point, they raise one and it starts again.
Why it can go over. If $499 of a $500 limit is used and the next booking’s discount is $10, that booking still gets its full $10 — we never shrink a discount a customer has already been shown, and we never leave an offer “running” that rejects everyone. The offer then stops at $509, and the card says so plainly. Owner-approved, 2026-09-22.

Number rules, binding. Uses is a whole number — integer only, minimum 1, no decimal point, and the phone keypad is digits-only. A provider cannot serve half a customer, and a fractional cap has no meaning. Discount is money — decimals allowed to that currency's own precision (two places for $ / € / £ / ₹ / C$, zero for a currency with no minor unit), the decimal keypad opens on a phone, and it is rounded and stored by the one shared money rule, never by a second one on the screen. Both reject zero and negatives: a limit of 0 would mean "nobody may claim this", which is what turning the switch off is for.
Responsive rules, binding. The limit rows are always one per line at every width — they are never placed side by side, because two switches on one line is where a provider mis-taps · the title and hint wrap; the switch never moves off the right edge and never wraps below · the stepper and money box stay left-aligned and grow to full width only below 520 px · the two meters are always full width · numbers never truncate or ellipsis — the card grows · every tap target is at least 44 px · the money symbol sits inside the field, never as a separate label · no horizontal scrolling at any width.
Why a switch and not an empty box. The first draft used a text field with “No limit” as placeholder text. That is ambiguous in exactly the wrong direction — an empty box can read as zero, i.e. “nobody may claim this”, which is the opposite of what it means. A switch says off = no limit in words, and only then asks for a number. It is also far better on a phone, where a placeholder disappears the moment the keyboard opens.

No “this offer has ended” message, no greyed-out badge, no empty space. A customer never learns an offer existed.
Customer rule. A stopped offer is absent, not disabled. It disappears from the search card, the service page, the provider page, the structured data we give search engines, and the phone assistant — all from the same one rule, with no extra code per surface. The phone assistant never says “only 3 left” — we cannot be accurate to the second, and a number we cannot honour is worse than saying nothing.

What this sheet does NOT cover, and must not be assumed from it. The edge cases listed on this sheet are examples only. The implementing session must work through every case it finds — races for the last use, a limit lowered below what is already used, a cancelled booking giving a use back, an offer deleted mid-checkout, a retried write, and anything else — and design for each one deliberately.
